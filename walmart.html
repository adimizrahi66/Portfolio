Background

We knew the Amazon shopper by heart. Walmart was a blank page.

Roundforest runs performance-based product-comparison sites. Shoppers land from Google or paid ads on a "Best [product]" page, compare options, and click through to a marketplace with an affiliate link. For years that marketplace was Amazon. In Q2 2026 the company decided to add Walmart – a second revenue channel with a different shopper, different price logic (Rollbacks, Walmart+) and a different trust model.

I was the designer on the initiative, responsible for understanding the Walmart audience and shaping the first landing pages before any traffic was bought.

Problem

A copy-paste of the Amazon page would be a guess, not a design.

The easy path was to swap the logo and the links. But every assumption on our Amazon pages – who the shopper is, what makes them click, what they need to feel confident – was unverified for Walmart. We needed answers before PPC started spending.

Business question

Which page formats convert for Walmart traffic?

Roundups, comparison grids, deals pages, deep reviews – which should we build first and for whom?

Design question · the real challenge

What does a Walmart shopper need to trust an affiliate page?

Who are they, how do they shop, and which trust signals are missing from Walmart itself that we could provide?

Research

I built a research plan and ran it myself.

With no budget for primary research, I wrote a research plan and synthesised public data: Walmart earnings and category reports, shopper-persona studies, UX statistics, affiliate benchmarks and Reddit threads from Walmart store associates. I audited Walmart's live product pages against Amazon's.

Market & categories

$648B revenue, e-commerce growing 21–22% YoY. Grocery 56% of digital, Health & Wellness fastest growing, Home and Electronics led by private labels (Mainstays, Onn).

Motivations

Navigability 70% · Omnichannel 66% · Fulfilment speed 64% · Price 61%. Price alone no longer wins – convenience is the loyalty battleground.

Pain points

Inventory accuracy ("nil picks"), a legacy 7-step mobile checkout, and a 3× conversion drop when AI-only checkout removed images, reviews and comparisons.

Generations

Gen Z leans Amazon for apparel; Millennials hold both Prime and Walmart+; Gen X and Boomers lean Walmart for health, home and staples and want simple, high-contrast layouts.

Insights

Walmart shoppers arrive knowing what they want. They need confirmation, not discovery.

I packaged the research into a 17-slide deck for Product, R&D, Design and PPC. Six insights drove everything that followed:

01

Reviews are Walmart's weak spot – that's our gap

Amazon's ratings are visible and interactive; Walmart shows AI-generated, pros-only summaries. Authentic, balanced reviews on our pages add real value.

02

"Load-bearing friction" converts

Comparison grids, product images and ratings aren't clutter. Removing them cut conversion 3×.

03

Deals-first, not discovery-first

Rollback and savings signals confirm a decision already half-made.

04

Private labels are a trust signal

Great Value, Equate and Onn are actively sought, not a fallback.

05

Mobile-first is non-negotiable

70% of affiliate conversions are mobile. Thumb-zone CTAs and sub-2s load times are features, not polish.

06

One design language won't scale

Boomers need clarity and large text; Millennials need speed and comparison; Gen Z wants visual exploration. One template cannot serve all three.

Strategy

Rank the page types, then pick the segments worth building for first.

I scored five affiliate landing-page formats against our audience and traffic model, and mapped a conversion funnel from search intent to the Walmart product page.

Format 1 Recommended

Category roundup

"Best [product] at Walmart" – matches high-intent search and has the highest affiliate CTR (9.2%).

Format 2 Recommended

Multi-marketplace grid

Side-by-side Walmart vs Amazon prices. Supports parallel scanning – the load-bearing friction shoppers want.

Format 3

Deep UGC reviews

3–7 products with pros, cons and real photos. Trusted by 90% of users, but shoppers are still in research mode.

Format 4

Single-product price comparison

Effective but volatile.

Risk: if Walmart is pricier, conversion dies instantly.

Format 5

Deals page

Rollbacks and flash deals.

Risk: high bounce outside promo windows.

1 / 5
Search / PPC→ Roundup page→ Compare + reviews→ Deal signal + sticky CTA→ Walmart product page
Decision

Start with the category roundup, built as a mobile-first template with two variants: Boomers (55+) – garden, home improvement – and Millennials (30+) – baby, sport, kitchen. These two segments lean Walmart the most and have the clearest, most different UX needs.

Solution

Same skeleton, two personalities.

Both templates share the structure the research called for: a deal-led hero, a scannable product list, trust blocks ("Every order comes with these guarantees", "How we find the best for you") and a thumb-zone "Check Price" CTA on every card. What changes is density, interaction and tone.

Template A · Boomer 55+

Clarity over choice

One product per row, large imagery, high-contrast text, "Arrives today" and "Best seller / Low stock" badges, "Rollback" savings shown as plain dollars. No compare mode – a single linear scroll, a "More top rated" section, and a help block at the bottom.

Template B · Millennial 30+

Compare, then commit

Visible price with strike-through, savings and delivery window on every card, "Add to compare" on each item with a floating compare tray, a "You may also like" row and a contact block – built for research-driven, efficiency-first shoppers.

Template B · iteration

Comparison that stays out of the way

The first Millennial version had no comparison. Following the "load-bearing friction" insight I added "Add to compare" on each card and a persistent compare tray under the header, then refined the selected state so it reads at a glance.

Card system

The product card carries the whole page, so I iterated it the most.

I started from Walmart's own listing language (Rollback, Best seller, Save $X, Arrives today) and tested how much of it a small mobile card can hold before it stops scanning. The final card leads with the image and rating, stacks three badges, then title, savings, delivery and a full-width CTA.

Explorations

From dense list rows to a single, badge-led card

Early drafts packed "Top pick" chips and brand names into tight rows. They felt like a spreadsheet. The chosen card gives the product image room, moves social proof (rating) to the top corner and keeps colour only for the badges and the CTA.

Handoff

Specs, states and a component library – ready for R&D on day one.

I delivered a "For dev" Figma page with both templates, every card state (best seller, low stock, rollback, compare-selected), spacing and type specs, and the trust-block components. In parallel, the research deck ended with prioritised action items per team:

R&D

Sub-2s page speed, live price and stock signals, 48px tap targets, A/B infrastructure for roundup vs grid and 20 vs 60 items per page.

Design

Segment templates, a reusable comparison grid, a UGC review block, a deals/urgency component set, and brand alignment with Walmart's palette and Spark iconography.

PPC

"Best [product] at Walmart" long-tail keywords, campaigns segmented by generation, Q4 seasonal budget surge and private-label comparison campaigns.

All teams

Align on Q4 strategy with a June kick-off.

Outcome

Three teams started from the same picture of the Walmart shopper.

1
research deck adopted as the shared baseline for Product, R&D and PPC.
2
segment-specific mobile templates, specced and handed to development.
5→1
landing-page formats evaluated, one clear starting point chosen.

The pages were scheduled to launch with the Q4 campaign window; performance data was not yet available when I left the company in July 2026.

Learned

Research is a product. Ship it to the people who need it.

Desk research can move a roadmap. Without a single interview, a well-sourced synthesis gave three teams a shared vocabulary and a ranked plan. The deck did more than the designs on their own.

Simplicity is not always the answer. The 3× drop for AI-only checkout was the clearest lesson: for this shopper, comparison and reviews are the confidence, not the clutter. I designed more density into the Millennial template than I would have by instinct.

Segment early. Picking two audiences instead of "everyone" made every decision – copy, density, CTA placement – easier to defend.
